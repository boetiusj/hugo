## Set Expectations

When both the contractor and the homeowner are clear on what’s being done, how it’s being done, and what to expect along the way, the job usually goes pretty smoothly. So why do painting projects sometimes end in frustration?

Most of the time, it comes down to mismatched expectations.

### On the Same Page

It’s rarely about someone cutting corners—more often, it’s a missed detail, a misunderstanding, or something that was never talked through in the first place. That’s why a little clarity up front goes a long way.

A clear, well-communicated agreement helps make sure everyone’s on the same page—so the work gets done right, with fewer surprises and a lot less stress.

> The Top 3 - Inclusions, Exclusions, Clarifications

### Define the Work

A solid painting agreement clearly outlines the essential elements of the project so there are no surprises later. A good agreement might include:

- Scope of Work – A detailed description of what will be painted (house body and trim), what will not be painted (shutters or gazebo) and any special notables (chimney flashing).  
- Responsibilities – Clarify who is responsible for what, such as surface prep, moving furniture or fixtures, protecting landscaping, or cleanup at the end of each day.  
- Timeline – Include the estimated number of working days to complete the project. This, of course, excludes weather delays.  
- Materials – Specify the products to be used, including paint brand, finish, number of coats, and any specialty primers or sealers.  
- Cost – List the total project cost, how and when payments are due, and whether any adjustments could apply (e.g., additional work orders or unforeseen repairs).

A well-written agreement like this sets expectations from the beginning—making the project smoother, more professional, and far less likely to end in dispute.

### Inclusions - What WILL Be Painted

This is the work that will be done. For example: “Paint house and garage only, including all previously painted doors, windows, soffits, fascia, shutters, and porch ceilings.” This section should also include surface preparation, paint types or brands, number of coats, and expected completion time.

Some examples of Inclusions:

- Scrape loose and failing paint.  
- Spot prime bare wood to improve topcoat adhesion.  
- Protect non-painted surfaces (brick, fencing, etc.) from drips and overspray.  
- Partial cans of paint may be left on the premises in sealed containers.

> A job well specified is half delivered

### Exclusions - What Will NOT Be Painted

This might seem like overkill, but it's crucial. If your fence, mailbox, doghouse, or porch swing won't be painted, let's clarify that. Don’t assume it’s obvious—surprise results can lead to frustration.

#### Avoiding Gray Doghouses

You might expect your white doghouse to stay white—so coming home to find it freshly coated in gray might not go over well. Be clear about what’s off-limits, even if it seems minor. At the same time, it’s okay to keep this section reasonable—your roof and driveway don’t need to be listed as excluded.

### Clarifications - What If

Details that fall outside the basic inclusions or exclusions—things like protecting delicate landscaping or coordinating access—should be clearly written into the agreement. Clarifications help ensure both the homeowner and contractor are on the same page before the job starts.

#### Windows Closed

It might sound like common sense, but it’s easy to overlook—especially during nice weather. Open windows can let in water spray or paint mist during power washing or painting, which can lead to an unexpected mess. It’s worth including a quick reminder in the agreement so everyone’s on the same page before the work begins.

#### Plants

Exterior painting often means working in and around flower beds, shrubs, and foundation plantings. Even with care, the process can be a little hard on landscaping—especially when ladders, drop cloths, and other equipment are involved.

It’s a good idea to talk through which plants might need extra attention. We’ll point out anything we’re concerned about, and if needed, arrange to have certain areas marked off or plants temporarily moved by a nursery. This helps the crew work efficiently while minimizing wear and tear on the landscaping.

### Communication

Clear, proactive communication between homeowner and contractor is essential to a smooth project—it sets expectations, prevents confusion, and builds trust from the start.

#### Suggestions

Some tasks are best handled by the homeowner ahead of time:

- Remove fragile or valuable items from work areas  
- Trim plants or shrubs that may block access  
- Keep pets secured  
- Ensure outdoor water and electrical access is available

#### Before Work Begins

An easy conversation (or walkaround) before the project starts can address key details: where equipment will be stored, how to handle pets or gates, where the crew will park. It’s also a good time to verify colors, finishes, and any last-minute questions about the scope of work.

#### The First Day

We’ll introduce the crew and go over what’s going to happen the first day or two (e.g., pressure washing, prep, masking). A clear first-day plan sets the tone for the job and shows the crew is organized and respectful of your space.

#### Tentative Start Date

Weather, job delays (customers often do ask for more work once we’re underway) - even sick kids can affect scheduling. Flexibility on both sides—and consistent communication—keeps the project on track even when conditions shift.

### Protect Everyone

#### A Clear Agreement Helps Everyone

When expectations are clearly defined—what’s included, what’s excluded, and what special requests are in place—everyone wins. There’s less stress, fewer surprises, and better working relationships.

#### It’s More Than a Legal Document

A painting agreement isn’t about covering yourself legally—though that’s a bonus. It’s about communication. The more clearly a project is described, the more likely it is to be delivered as expected. And as they say: a job well specified is half delivered.
